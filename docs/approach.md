# Grid learning: a world model for Clash Royale with the structure the game has

## 1. Purpose

CR-234 is a Clash Royale playing bot, but the bot is the instrument rather than the goal. The question is how a learned system comes to hold a model of a world it only partly observes, and whether committing to the structure the world actually has makes that model cheaper to learn, easier to inspect, and better to plan with than a model that must discover the structure on its own. Clash Royale suits the question. Its state is continuous in position and discrete in almost everything else, and a player sees most but not all of it. The simulator exposes its own hidden state for controlled experiments. Agreement with the real game remains an empirical requirement; simulated hidden state is not ground truth for real matches.

The repository is laid out to match. `sim/` is the Python simulator (20 Hz tick, 18 by 32 tile arena, 1000 game units per tile), with regression tests in `tests/`. `data/` builds the single-schema `cards.json` from ClashStrategic stats and RoyaleAPI patch notes for all card levels. `train/` holds the behavior-cloning transformer over placement sequences and the proximal policy optimization (PPO) code. The RoyaleAPI replay scraper and the official Supercell API client live in a separate private repository, since collection details stay private. `docs/` holds this text and `figures.ipynb`, which produces the figures.

This document presents the derivation behind the approach, which we call grid learning, then the comparison arms, the data and fidelity work, and a roadmap.

## 2. The game as a dynamical system

A match runs 3 minutes of regulation and up to 2 minutes of overtime at 20 ticks per second. Elixir accrues at one unit per 2.8 s, doubled after the first two minutes and tripled in the final minute of overtime, capped at 10. Each player has an 8-card deck, holds 4 in hand, and a played card goes to the back of a deterministic cycle. Each side has two crown towers and a king tower. The current simulator contains 123 card definitions plus 4 tower troops, 42 evolution definitions and 17 hero definitions, alongside champions with activatable abilities. This is an implementation inventory, not a claim that every released mechanic or special-event card is fully verified.

We treat the match as a discrete-time dynamical system. The state s_t holds an entity multiset, where each entity carries type, team, level, continuous position, hit points, attack cooldown phase, current target, status effects, and any per-mechanic counters; a low-dimensional global block holding time, both elixirs, both hands and queues, tower hit points, king activation, and the pending deploy queue; and the two players' actions a_t^b and a_t^r. Randomness in the real game is minor (spawn jitter, a small deployment delay), so to first order

s_{t+1} = F(s_t, a_t^b, a_t^r),

and the simulator is an executable F that can be queried without limit. At 24K ticks per second, one core runs roughly 24K regulation-length games per hour.

A player does not see s_t. The observation is o_t = H(s_t), where H drops the opponent's elixir, hand, and queue, quantizes hit points to bars, and hides cooldown phases. The hidden part is nevertheless recoverable. Opponent elixir is a deterministic function of elapsed time and the opponent's observed plays: it starts at 5, accrues at the known rate, is capped at 10, and each visible deployment subtracts a known cost. The cap makes the map from history to elixir saturating but not ambiguous. Every played card is visible and goes to the back of the queue, so after the opponent's eighth distinct card the deck is known and the queue order is the play order of the last four cards; the hand is exactly determined from then on. The one wrinkle is that a deployed champion leaves the cycle while it lives, which is itself an observed event. Cooldown phases are fixed by the first observed attack of each unit. In control-theoretic terms the system is observable: two different states produce different observation sequences within finite time, and an observer, a dynamical system that consumes observations and known inputs and outputs a state estimate, can converge to the true state in finite time. The linear theory is Luenberger's (1971); the nonlinear analogue whose structure we borrow is the Kazantzis and Kravaris (1998) observer, and Bernard, Andrieu and Astolfi (2022) survey the design space.

The world learner in this program is therefore two learned objects: a nonlinear observer that turns the observation stream into an estimate of s_t, and a transition kernel that approximates F. Both are trained supervised, because the simulator supplies the hidden state for every tick. This is the main departure from the world-model literature in reinforcement learning, where MuZero (Schrittwieser et al. 2020), DreamerV3 (Hafner et al. 2023), IRIS (Micheli et al. 2023) and TD-MPC2 (Hansen et al. 2024) learn a latent state that has no ground truth and is judged only by the return it supports. Here the latent is the state itself, and every part of it can be checked.

## 3. The structure the transition rule has

Grid learning builds in three properties of F. The first is locality. Per tick, an entity's update depends only on the entities within its sight range, which lies between 5.5 and 9.5 tiles across all cards, and on its current target, which it acquired within that range. Spells act within a bounded radius of at most about 5.5 tiles. Movement per tick is at most 0.1 tile (the fastest unit moves 120 tiles per minute), and collision radii are about half a tile. So F is a local rule with a finite interaction radius R of about 10 tiles, and the locality is exact: nothing farther than R can affect a cell within one tick. The only non-local quantities are the small global block and, through it, the position of the tower a unit is walking toward, which is static geometry together with an alive flag. Figure 1 draws R to scale: centered on the left bridge it reaches both crown towers in that lane but neither king tower, a lane rather than the field.

![Arena with interaction radius](fig/arena_radius.png)

*Figure 1. The 18 by 32 tile arena with river, bridges, tower footprints and deploy zones. The circle is the interaction radius R = 10 tiles around a unit on the left bridge; the mesh shows the half-tile raster.*

The second property is translation equivariance. The same rule applies at every location; what differs between locations is the static geometry (river, bridges, tower footprints, deploy zones, arena edges), which enters the rule as an input, not as part of it. This is what lets a uniform kernel learn the geometry of a finite world and generalize to changed geometry: a destroyed tower is a change in the geometry channel, and the same weights apply. A cellular automaton rule (Mordvintsev et al. 2020) and a Neural GPU (Kaiser and Sutskever 2016) generalize to configurations and sizes they were never trained on by the same mechanism, and the graph simulators of Battaglia et al. (2016) and Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. (2020) make the same commitment on particles rather than cells.

The third property is a discrete symmetry. The arena is symmetric under left-right mirroring and under 180-degree rotation combined with a team swap, together the dihedral group D2. A rule that respects this symmetry can be enforced by weight sharing over the group, as in group-equivariant convolution (Cohen and Welling 2016), or by averaging predictions over the four transformed inputs. Either way, mirrored interactions are learned once.

A transformer trained on Othello move sequences forms an internal board representation recoverable by probes (Li et al. 2023), linearly in the right coordinates (Nanda, Lee and Wattenberg 2023). But the same kind of model can pass next-token tests while holding an incoherent world model that breaks under small perturbations (Vafa et al. 2024), and transformers trained to simulate automata learn shallow shortcuts that fail off-distribution (Liu et al. 2023). Grid learning is the opposite bet: give the model the locality, equivariance and symmetry the game has, so that capacity is spent on the content of the rule rather than on rediscovering its form, and what it learned is readable cell by cell.

## 4. Grid learning

### 4.1 Rasterization

The field is rasterized at half-tile resolution into 36 by 64 = 2304 cells. Each cell carries the static geometry channels and a permutation-invariant summary of the entities inside it: the sum of embeddings of (type, team, level, hit point fraction, sub-cell offset, status flags, cooldown phase). The sum-of-embeddings form is the Deep Sets construction (Zaheer et al. 2017) and lets a cell hold a bag of any size. The result is image-like, yet lossless for positions (the offset channels keep the continuous coordinates) and for stacking (a Skeleton Army that piles seven skeletons into one half-tile cell is represented exactly). Figure 2 shows a simulator state and its raster.

![Simulator state and raster](fig/sim_raster.png)

*Figure 2. Left: simulator entities at 60 s, a Giant and Musketeer push on the left met by a Skeleton Army and Knight, a Hog Rider against Archers on the right; tower hit points on the footprints. Right: entity count per half-tile cell. Seven skeletons share one cell; the per-cell bag keeps them all.*

### 4.2 The kernel

The transition kernel is a stack of local attention layers over the raster. Neighborhood attention (Hassani et al. 2023) lets each cell attend to the w by w cells around it, so after L layers the receptive radius is L(w-1)/2 cells. With R = 10 tiles = 20 cells, this needs L(w-1)/2 >= 20; Figure 3 marks the minimal configurations, for example w = 7 with L = 7 or w = 11 with L = 4. A few global tokens carry the global block, both hands and the step size k described next, and are read by every layer, so the small non-local part of F reaches every cell without breaking the locality of the rest.

![Receptive field coverage](fig/receptive_field.png)

*Figure 3. Left: receptive radius of a neighborhood-attention stack against depth for several window sizes; stars mark the minimal depth that covers R. Right: attention pairs per cell for each minimal configuration.*

The output at each cell is discrete: type, team, hit point bucket and offset bucket for each entity, emitted into a fixed number of slots in a canonical order with a count token, trained with cross-entropy. Discrete targets beat regression here because most of the state is discrete and the ambiguity that exists (which of two equidistant targets a unit picks) is a distribution over outcomes, not a mean.

Weights are shared across all 2304 cells. One tick of one game is therefore 2304 supervised examples, and a full regulation game is about 8.3 million. Figure 4 puts this against the roughly 40 decision labels a human replay yields; the entire clean human dataset of 168K 1v1 battles carries about as many decision labels as a single simulated game carries cell labels. Most cells are empty and the examples are correlated, so the effective count is lower, but the gap is orders of magnitude, and it is where minimal training comes from: a kernel with w = 7, L = 7 and width 128 has under two million parameters, and the data to train it is free.

![Supervision density](fig/supervision_density.png)

*Figure 4. Left: supervised examples per game at four granularities. Right: cumulative examples against number of games for human decision labels and simulated cell labels.*

One training detail carries over from learned physics simulators: Sanchez-Gonzalez et al. (2020) found that small input noise during training is the main lever for stable long rollouts, because the model then sees the kind of slightly wrong state it will produce itself, and we perturb offsets and hit point buckets for the same reason. Rollouts are also evaluated on held-out geometry (a destroyed tower, a rarely used deploy position) to check that equivariance, not memorized position, is doing the work.

### 4.3 Step size and compounding error

Tick-level rollouts are too fine for planning over 10 to 30 s: a 30 s horizon is 600 compositions. The same kernel is conditioned on a step size k in {1, 4, 16, 64} ticks and trained on (s_t, s_{t+k}) pairs, with a consistency term that penalizes disagreement between the direct 2k prediction and the composition of two k predictions, which ties the scales together and gives the coarse kernel signal from the fine one.

The reason coarse steps help is the autoregressive error bound. Let F be the true k-tick map with Lipschitz constant L_F in some metric d on states, and let the learned map G satisfy d(G(s), F(s)) <= eps for all s. Write e_T = d(G^T(s), F^T(s)) for the error after T compositions, with e_0 = 0. By the triangle inequality,

e_{T+1} <= d(G(G^T s), F(G^T s)) + d(F(G^T s), F(F^T s)) <= eps + L_F e_T,

and unrolling gives

e_T <= eps (1 + L_F + ... + L_F^{T-1}) = eps (L_F^T - 1)/(L_F - 1),

which reduces to T eps when L_F = 1. For a horizon of H seconds with step k, the number of compositions is T = 20H/k. Figure 5 plots the compounding factor (L_F^T - 1)/(L_F - 1) against H for each k. The factor is exponential in T, and T is inversely proportional to k, so a coarse kernel wins whenever its one-step error eps_k grows more slowly with k than the compounding factor shrinks, which is by a large margin at any L_F above one.

![Compounding error bound](fig/compounding.png)

*Figure 5. Compounding factor of the error bound against horizon for step sizes of 1, 4, 16 and 64 ticks at three values of L_F, held fixed across k; vertical lines mark 10 and 30 s.*

The bound also says where coarse steps are unsafe. L_F is a property of the dynamics and depends on k: in the worst case the k-tick map is as sensitive as k compositions of the tick map and nothing is gained. It is near one on a quiet field, where units walk in straight lines and towers do nothing, and large near an interaction, where a target switch or a death changes everything downstream. The planner therefore uses coarse steps where the field is quiet (no enemy within R of any unit, no pending deployment) and fine steps near interactions. Both eps_k and the effective L_F are measured on the simulator per k.

### 4.4 The observer

The observer is the same network with a predict-then-correct split, the structure of a Luenberger observer. One sub-step applies the transition kernel to the current estimate of the full state; the second attends over the new observation raster (hit point bars, no cooldown phases, opponent's global block masked) and corrects the estimate. It is trained with cross-entropy against the simulator's hidden state, so the per-cell loss on the corrected estimate is the observer error, and its convergence can be checked against Section 2: opponent elixir should be exact from the first tick, the opponent's hand exact after the eighth distinct card, cooldown phases exact after each unit's first attack.

## 5. Planning and measuring the planning gap

Decisions in Clash Royale are sparse (about 20 per player per game) and the action space is small after pruning. A player holds 4 cards, each playable on the feasible tiles of the deploy zone, and mirroring halves the count; in practice a handful of canonical placements per card (behind the king, at the bridge, in front of a tower, adjacent to the current threat) captures the decisions that matter. Inference-time experimentation is then a shallow beam search or Monte Carlo tree search in the sense of Kocsis and Szepesvári (2006) over two to three decisions, rolling the kernel forward from the current estimate under each candidate action sequence, with a value read from tower hit points and the elixir differential. At k = 64 ticks, a 30 s rollout is 10 kernel evaluations, so a beam of tens of candidates costs a few hundred forward passes per decision. The rollouts live in the raster, so a questionable decision can be inspected as a sequence of predicted boards rather than an opaque latent.

Because the oracle F exists, the planning gap is measurable: the same planner run with the true simulator and with the learned kernel as its model, against the same opponents, differs in win rate by the world-model error alone. The fidelity metrics feeding this are per-cell exactness of the k-tick prediction, tower hit point error at 10, 30 and 60 s horizons, and win prediction from mid-game states, each reported per step size and per data regime.

The data regimes, in order of cost, are unlimited simulator rollouts under scripted and behavior-cloned policies, which teach the rule; human replays replayed through the simulator, which supply the realistic state distribution; and the agent's own games, which supply the states its planning actually visits.

The recorded outcome is the ground truth in all of this, even where the simulator disagrees, and the first learned component treats the simulator accordingly: as a witness whose trust is learned. `train/calibratedHead.py` fits a logistic head for the probability that the replayed side wins from the recorded decks, levels and tower troops together with the simulator's verdict, crown margin, end time and premature flag, on six assessed 2,000-game samples with the two most recent held out (about 8,000 training and 4,000 test games). On the held-out games the simulator's bare verdict is right 64.5% of the time but is not a probability (expected calibration error 0.345, log loss 1.64); a head on the verdict alone is right 63.8% with calibration error 0.030; the head with decks and verdict is right 65.0% with calibration error 0.019 and log loss 0.623, against 0.688 for decks alone, so the simulator carries most of the signal and the head makes it usable as a probability. The head's fitted weights say how it trusts the simulator: the crown margin carries the weight (1.40 per three-crown margin against 0.25 for the sign of the verdict), and a verdict from a game the simulator ended early is discounted by 0.58, so an early simulated ending is a weaker witness. Most telling, the per-card correction the combined head adds on top of the deck-only weights has rank correlation minus 0.54 with the per-card bias table (simulated minus recorded holder win rate) on the training games and minus 0.47 with the same table measured only on the held-out games: without ever seeing that table, the head discounts the holders of the evolved Inferno Dragon, the evolved Mega Knight, the Boss Bandit, the Golem and the evolved P.E.K.K.A, the cards the simulator over-favours, and credits Goblinstein, the Cannon, the evolved Tesla and the hero Ice Golem, the cards it under-favours. This is the calibrated-decision layer's job: the simulator remains the biased prior, the recorded outcomes are the labels, and the disagreement between them is itself a map of where the simulator most needs fidelity work. Three tests plant a simulator bias in synthetic data and check that the head's corrections recover it and that the combined model is better calibrated than the verdict alone; the report is `local/calibration/head1.json`.

The second learned component moves from the game to the decision. `train/decisionStates.py` wraps the shipped replay path (the judge's own `Game` class, patched for one run) and records, at every recorded card play, the state the acting player saw as `train/feats.featurize` sees it, the play, the recorded winner and the simulator's winner of the same replay: 102,429 decisions from the 2,000 games of one assessed sample, extracted in five minutes with two workers. `train/decisionHead.py` fits logistic heads for the probability that the actor wins the recorded game, held out by whole games (25%, 28,577 decisions): from the simulated board and the play alone, from the simulator's verdict alone with a weight per game phase, and from both, beside the bare verdict. The result is not what a planner would hope for. The simulated board at the moment of a decision predicts the recorded winner at 57% overall (54% in the first minute, 59% in the last minute of regulation, 58% in overtime) even with the actor's tower health lead and towers standing added as explicit features, while the simulator's terminal verdict, a constant per game, predicts it at 64%; adding the board to the verdict makes the held-out head slightly worse (63.0%, log loss 0.663 against 0.654), and the weight the joint head puts on the verdict falls from 0.61 in the first minute to 0.18 in overtime because the late simulated board already encodes the simulated ending. The witness table explains the gap: the simulated tower health lead at a decision predicts the simulator's own verdict at 51%, 63%, 74% and 71% by phase, so even inside the simulation the mid-game board decides little, and it predicts the recorded winner at 52%, 57%, 58% and 56%; a simulated crown lead, a tower already down in the simulation between two and three minutes in, is on the recorded winner's side only 60% of the time (16,766 decisions). The recorded games that are still running late are also the ones the simulator gets wrong most (verdict agreement 65% for decisions in the first minute, 61% in the last regulation minute, 59% in overtime). Two readings follow. For the planner, the simulator's value lies in its rollouts to the end, not in reading its intermediate board; a decision head must be trained on rollout outcomes and real outcomes, not on the reconstructed state, which is what the counterfactual-rollout step is for. For fidelity, an early simulated tower fall is the least trustworthy event the simulator produces, in line with the negative weight the game-level head gave to early endings, so the premature-ending rate is the metric most worth driving down. Seven tests cover the extractor on synthetic plays through the real replay entry point (one record per card play from the actor's side, drawn games unlabelled, the pre-play state) and the head on synthetic decisions with a planted late board signal and a planted verdict agreement (the earliest informative phase, the falling verdict weight, the witness table); the report is `local/calibration/decisionHead1.json`.

The third learned component is the one this testbed exists for: learning a policy directly from production traces whose feedback is evaluative. Every replay is one trajectory of a human policy through an environment nobody can recreate (the opponent was a person, the simulator is a biased model of the rest), and the only feedback is the recorded winner, so environment replay in the style of GRPO (rerun the policy from the recorded state, sample a group of plays, rank them) is unavailable in the real environment. `train/traceRl.py` compares the ways of turning the single recorded trajectory into an update, on 799,645 decisions from 15,999 games of eight assessed samples (the decision records now carry the replay's forced hand, so a play is one of the four cards in hand on one of 48 deploy cells), with 25% of the games held out whole (200,119 decisions): behaviour cloning (the outcome ignored); single-trajectory REINFORCE on the winner with the constant baseline one half (signed weights, no constraint); a one-sample-per-prompt clipped-ratio update whose advantage is the outcome minus a state-value baseline fit on real outcomes and whose ratio is anchored to the frozen behaviour policy; advantage-weighted regression with the same baseline; and environment replay in the world model, where `train/counterfactual.py` replays the recorded game with one play replaced (another hand card at the same tile, or the same card on another cell), scores the simulated result for the actor (crown difference plus tower health margin), and the group-relative advantages of 1,500 games' menus (37,314 groups, 149,256 full replays, 66 minutes on 30 devbox cores) drive the same clipped update, once with trust one and once weighted by the decision head's trust in the simulator per game phase. The evaluations on the held-out games are the winner gap (mean log-probability of the recorded play in won games minus lost games), agreement with winners' and losers' plays, the KL divergence to the behaviour policy, and a Q-eval, in which a P(win | state, play) model fit on real outcomes (56.3% held-out accuracy, log loss 0.688) scores each policy's play distribution over the plays with support in the data. Behaviour cloning has a winner gap of 0.014 (winners' plays are slightly more typical). The constrained single-trajectory updates widen it at small cost: the clipped-ratio update to 0.025 at KL 0.055, advantage-weighted regression to 0.036 at KL 0.203, both keeping 99.5% of their mass on supported plays and both closing the phase where the behaviour policy is most wrong-footed (the last minute of regulation, gap minus 0.031 for behaviour cloning, minus 0.016 and minus 0.001 after the two updates); their Q-eval rises by 0.0011 and 0.0014 in win probability, within the head's resolution. Unconstrained REINFORCE collapses (entropy 3.27 to 0.12, KL 8.7, agreement with the recorded card 51% to 23%, 30% of its mass off supported plays) and the direct-method Q-eval rewards the collapse (0.518 against 0.486 for behaviour cloning) because the policy concentrates on the head's argmax where the data cannot contradict it: the off-policy estimate is only meaningful where the constraint holds, which is the argument for the constraint. The world-model arm moves the policy as far as the constrained updates (KL 0.15) but in another direction: its winner gap stays at 0.013 to 0.014, its Q-eval matches the constrained updates (0.4873), and its agreement with the recorded plays falls for winners and losers alike (51% to 48%). The world model's judgement is not empty: the recorded play beats its group mean by more in games the actor won (advantage 0.073 against 0.029, and the recorded play is the best of its menu 41% of the time for winners and 36% for losers, chance 25%), but the correlation between that judgement and the real outcome is 0.03 per decision (0.05 in the first minute, 0.02 in the last minute of regulation, 0.07 in overtime), and the trust weighting changes nothing measurable because it only rescales phases the update already treats alike. The reading for the research question: from one trajectory per game and one bit of feedback, a value baseline plus a behaviour constraint extracts a small, consistent signal without recreating anything; the world model's counterfactuals at the simulator's current fidelity (65% winner agreement) carry a weakly correlated signal the real-outcome methods do not need, and their value is to be measured again on the same held-out games as fidelity improves. A first run with over-fitted heads (60 epochs, log loss above the constant predictor) gave the same ordering (behaviour cloning 0.018, clipped 0.029, weighted 0.039, world model 0.015). Six tests plant a decision rule the synthetic behaviour follows 60% of the time and draw the winner from the two players' shares of right plays: every outcome-driven update moves toward the right card, the constrained ones stay near the behaviour policy while REINFORCE drifts, an oracle world model's group advantages learn the rule, zero trust changes nothing, the rollout of the recorded play reproduces the plain replay exactly, and a relocated placement is one decision. Reports: `local/traceRlRound/traceRl2.json` and `local/traceRlRound/cfAgreement.json`.

The fourth step measures the world model's bias directly, in three ways, on the same 799,645 decisions (25% of the 15,999 games held out whole, 200,119 decisions of 3,976 games) and the same 1,500-game counterfactual set. First, `train/worldModelBias.py` iterates the clipped update with its anchor refreshed every five epochs, the iterated trust region a world model permits because it can be queried again, and evaluates each arm at 30, 60 and 90 epochs; the same iteration on the real-outcome advantages (two batch orders each for the world model and the clipped real update), advantage-weighted regression, and the clipped update on outcomes flipped for a random half of the training games (the noise floor) are the references. With a refreshed anchor the fixed advantages drive every clipped arm the same distance whatever they say: KL to behaviour cloning 0.5 to 1.2 at 30 epochs, 1.4 to 4.7 at 60 and about 11 at 90, where all of them, the noise arm included, have collapsed (entropy 3.27 to below 0.12, a third to two thirds of the mass off supported plays, winner gaps of plus 1.7 to 2.1 for the real update, minus 1.6 to 2.3 for the world model and minus 1.9 for noise), so the distance is set by the optimiser and only the direction carries information. At 30 epochs the world-model arm (KL 1.15 and 1.08) has a winner gap of 0.019 and 0.009 against 0.014 for behaviour cloning, agreement with the recorded card down from 51% to 37% for winners and losers alike, and a Q-eval of 0.4897, above the noise arm's 0.4861 and below the real update's 0.4919 at KL 0.56 and 0.4943 at KL 1.43; advantage-weighted regression, which has no ratio to re-anchor, reaches KL 0.36 and a winner gap of 0.072 at 90 epochs. The direction is read from the per-state preference shift, the log ratio of the policy to behaviour cloning over the menu of 192 plays on 20,000 held-out states, as the mean cosine between arms. At 30 epochs the world model agrees with itself across batch orders at 0.94 at the card level and the real update with itself at 0.78; the two real-outcome methods agree at 0.45; the world model agrees with the real update at 0.26 and with the noise arm at 0.14, which is also the real update's agreement with noise. Over the full menu the rank correlation is 0.09 for the world model against the real update, 0.04 to 0.05 against noise and 0.48 between the two real methods. By 60 epochs the shared collapse mode dominates (world model against real update 0.47, against noise 0.43) and by 90 every pairing of collapsed arms exceeds 0.65. So the world model's update direction is systematic, reproducible to 0.94 across batch orders, and mostly its own: its excess agreement with the real-outcome direction over the noise floor (0.12 at the card level) is a third of the excess between two real-outcome methods (0.32), and at the menu level it is barely above the floor. Second, `train/cardSplit.py` says what that direction is. For each of 115 card variants with at least 100 recorded plays among the 37,314 counterfactual groups, the mean world-model advantage of the recorded play (its return minus its menu's mean) is set against the mean real advantage (the outcome minus a value baseline fit on the 14,499 games outside the rollout set), both standardised over decisions. Across cards the two do not agree (Spearman 0.13, permutation p 0.16), and the per-card gap, world model minus real, has rank correlation minus 0.51 (p below 0.0005, 108 cards) with the per-card correction of the game-level calibrated head and 0.12 (p 0.21) with the raw holder bias table: the 21 cards the head discounts most have a mean gap of plus 0.29 and the 21 it credits most minus 0.11. The world model over-rates recorded plays of the evolved and plain Mega Knight (0.81, 0.72), the P.E.K.K.A (0.80), the Elite Barbarians (0.69), Sparky (0.60), the Inferno Dragon (0.58) and the Golem (0.46) and under-rates spells and cycle cards (Elixir Collector minus 0.58, Heal Spirit minus 0.56, Vines minus 0.48, Zap minus 0.46, Tornado minus 0.35, The Log minus 0.29), while the real advantages of those cards are flat: the card-level bias the calibrated head found at the game level is present in the counterfactual judgement of single decisions, and it is the systematic direction the first measure saw. Third, `train/jointQ.py` fits the decision-level P(win | state, play) head jointly on the real labels and on the rollout labels of the training games (111,868 rows in 27,967 menus), either as the simulated winner (a soft label about the level) or as the return relative to the menu mean (a constraint only on how a state's plays compare), with a trust weight per rollout row swept from 0.3 to 30, alone and scaled per game phase by the decision head's verdict weights, and reports the paired per-game log-loss difference to the real-only head over the 3,976 held-out games. The real-only head has log loss 0.6879 and accuracy 56.6% (a second seed 0.6877); no trust helps beyond seed noise (the best arm, relative labels at trust 0.3 with phase weights, minus 0.0006 plus or minus 0.0003, the seed-to-seed difference being 0.0000 plus or minus 0.0006), the winner labels hurt from trust 1 (plus 0.0021 plus or minus 0.0009, plus 0.015 at 3, plus 0.061 at 10) and the relative labels from trust 3 (plus 0.0037 plus or minus 0.0006, plus 0.011 at 10), and the phase weights soften the damage (0.737 against 0.754 at trust 10) without creating a gain. What the head does absorb is the world model's ranking: the real-only head's within-menu rank agreement with the simulator on the held-out menus is 0.03 (top play agreement 27%, chance 25%), the relative labels at trust 0.3 raise it to 0.27 (42%) and at trust 1 to 0.34 (47%) at no cost in real log loss. The three measures agree: the world model's counterfactual judgement is reproducible, orthogonal to what predicts the recorded outcome, and biased toward the cards the simulator already over-favours; at this fidelity it can be absorbed at low trust without harm but supplies no outcome signal, and the per-card gap table is the fidelity worklist. Twelve tests plant the structures: a card-only logit change is read back as the menu shift, flipped outcomes flip whole games, an oracle world model drifts in the real direction above the noise floor, planted per-card offsets rank with a planted head table, zero trust reproduces the real-only head to the bit, and an oracle's rollout labels improve the head on held-out real outcomes. Reports: `local/traceRlRound/worldModelBias.json`, `cardSplit.json` and `jointQ.json`.

The fifth step measures the recorded side of that table on a different scale. The official Clash Royale API serves battle logs (decks, levels, tower troops, crowns, tower health, winner) but no replays, and a polite collector has been streaming them since 2026-09-28; a snapshot of 6,454,828 rows on 2026-09-30 converts to 4,230,459 one-versus-one games, of which 3,826,119 are played with the players' own decks under standard rules (Ranked 1,370,807; trophy-road and legacy ladder 2,450,086; tournaments 5,226; draft, event-deck and modifier modes excluded). `train/streamDeck.py` fits the deck-only head of `train/calibratedHead.py` (the same one-hot deck difference, tower troops, level and king differences, no simulator terms because there are none) on the games of the days before 2026-09-29 (3,461,521) and scores the held-out day (347,393 games), beside a random split by battle key of the same size. At this scale the deck head reaches log loss 0.671 and 57.5% (0.663 and 59.0% on the random split, calibration error 0.012), against 0.688 and 55.3% for the same head fit on 8,000 replay-set games, and a matchup head that adds a full table of cross-side card pairs (antisymmetric) and same-side pairs (symmetric), its penalty chosen on the last training day, reaches 0.645 and 62.1% (0.634 and 63.4% random), on other games than the 64.5% of the simulator's verdict on the replay set. The gain sits on ladder games, where the mean card level difference (weight 0.73 per level) and the deck decide; on Ranked games at matched rating the deck head is worth 0.002 in log loss over the constant (53.3%) and the matchup head 0.027 (59.3%), and the same heads scored on the replay set's 11,998 games give 0.697 (no better than a constant) and 0.674 (58.4%). The per-card recorded holder win rate, the recorded half of the bias table, now has a standard error near 0.002 for all 178 cards. Over all standard modes it does not rank like the replay set's (Spearman minus 0.11 on 157 cards, 47 moved by more than three standard errors) because the ladder population holds different cards at different levels: the Barbarian Hut's holders win 33%, Three Musketeers' 38%, against 46% and 52% on Ranked. On Ranked alone (1,360,672 games) the ranks agree (Spearman 0.64) and five cards moved, Poison, Skeleton Army, Knight and Firecracker up and the evolved Elite Barbarians down after their 2026-09-08 nerf, where 0.4 false positives are expected. Replacing the replay set's recorded half by the Ranked stream rates leaves the bias table in order (Spearman 0.92 with the published table, 8 of the 10 most distrusted and 8 of the 10 most trusted cards unchanged, the head's correction still at minus 0.55 against it), and the reason is the spread: the simulated holder rates of the 156 cards run from 0.31 to 0.68 (standard deviation 0.081) where the real Ranked rates run from 0.44 to 0.55 (0.017), and the simulator's card ranking correlates 0.19 with the real one. The distrust list stands, the simulator exaggerates card-level outcome effects about four-fold, and the recorded half was never the noisy part that mattered. For the decision-level work the stream supplies a deck prior P(win | decks, levels), refit daily (a fit takes under a minute; the held-out day is 0.01 worse than the random split, the size of the drift), as the value baseline and as an input of the calibrated head in place of the deck block learned from 8,000 games; it cannot supply placements, hero variants or player ratings, so it labels nothing at the decision level and cannot separate a card's strength from its holders' without a rating column. Eight tests plant a card effect and a counter in synthetic battle rows and check the feature equality with the replay-set head, the day and key splits, the recovered weights and rates, the antisymmetry of the matchup head and the hero folding. Reports: `local/streamRound/streamDeck.json` and `streamRound.json`.

The sixth step returns to learning from the traces, now at the scale of the whole replay corpus and with the three single-trajectory recipes the research question named, whose papers have since appeared: FlashREINFORCE (Hu et al. 2026), SAO (Hou et al. 2026) and BPCO (Qi, Zhou and Lee 2026). `train/corpusStates.py` extracts decision states through the shipped replay path from every eligible game of the private corpus (285,372 one-versus-one standard-mode games with a replay, after removing the 100,256 ids of the frozen fidelity samples), in battle-time order, and adds per decision what the actor cannot see: the opponent's hand and next card in the replay's reconstructed cycle and, for the last 49,995 games, a ten-second no-play rollout of the simulator from the decision state (a copy of the game in which neither side plays; the copy must duplicate the projectile callbacks and restore the random state and unit counters, otherwise the replay itself changes). This gives 14,015,761 decisions of 285,323 games in about three hours on 30 devbox cores.

`train/singleTraj.py` implements the recipes at the decision level. A game is two trajectories, one per player; a decision is two tokens (a card from the forced hand, then a deploy cell given the card); the opponent's plays are observations that the skip-observation estimator steps over; the reward is the recorded outcome. FlashREINFORCE centres the outcome on the batch mean and weights token log-probabilities by importance ratios against the behaviour policy, admitting a whole trajectory only if its mean sampled-token Bernoulli divergence is below 0.003, with no critic and no clipping. SAO updates a critic twice per actor update, computes generalized advantage estimation (Schulman et al. 2016) over the actor's own tokens with the length-adaptive parameter 1 - 1/(1.5 T), and masks tokens whose ratio leaves (0.7, 6). BPCO masks tokens whose probability moved by more than 0.2 in the direction of the advantage (the binary total-variation rule of Qi et al. 2026), bounds the critic to the reward range with a scaled arctangent, regresses it on the Monte Carlo outcome, keeps advantages unnormalised and uses 1 - 1/(0.4 T); its critic may also see privileged inputs: the opponent's hand, the no-play rollout, or the simulator's verdict on the recorded continuation, which depends on the actor's own plays and is therefore not a valid baseline. Games before 2 September (43,598) fit the behaviour clone every arm starts from and pretrain the critics; the 217,370 games of 2 to 5 September are consumed once in time order, 64 games per batch and one actor update per batch; the 24,355 games of 6 September are held out (the corpus has no final week with volume), half of them fitting a real-outcome Q model (56.8% held-out accuracy, log loss 0.682 against 0.693 for the constant) and half scoring. The human behaviour probabilities were never stored, so every ratio and trust region uses a behaviour-cloning estimate, by default one fit on the other half of the stream games. The Q-eval is reported support-restricted, pessimistic (mass off the supported plays scored at the state's worst supported play) and direct, and an arm is flagged, by a rule registered before the first run, if the direct estimate gains more than 0.002 over the pessimistic one or support mass falls by more than a point; under the same registration, gains in the restricted or pessimistic estimate with support mass within a point of the clone's count as improvements.

The production-trace twist breaks FlashREINFORCE. At the start the estimate differs from the actor by a mean Bernoulli divergence of 0.010 per token, 3.3 times the threshold (0.15% of first-day trajectories would pass at the start), and by 0.021 on average over the first day as the actor drifts, so the gate admits 0.03% of trajectories on the first day and fewer later; the rare admitted trajectories still move the policy (KL 0.39 to the clone, because Adam normalises the sparse gradients to full-size steps) and lower the Q-eval by 0.0014, and the estimate fit on the same games, a temperature of 0.8 or 1.25, or a threshold of 0.005 change nothing (-0.0019 to -0.0039). Only with the actor's own starting point or an online clone as the estimate, the analogue of stored sampler probabilities, does the gate admit trajectories (15% and 12% on the first day), and it closes within a day (0.3% and 0.1% on the second) because nothing refreshes the sampler offline: the gate then caps the total drift from a fixed reference; the gain is 0.0034 to 0.0035. Without the gate the unclipped ratios collapse the policy onto the plays the Q model favours within the supported set (entropy 3.38 to 1.29), which the Q-eval rewards (+0.073). The registered rule counts that gain as an improvement (support mass 0.9996, the direct and pessimistic gains equal) and so misses the collapse; it shows only in checks added after the first runs: 21% of the policy's mass on each state's Q-argmax play against 0.3% for the clone, and 1.2 points of mass lost under a stricter support count (plays seen at least 100 times).

The token masks with a critic are insensitive to the estimate: over six estimates (cross-fitted, same games, the starting point, online, temperatures 0.8 and 1.25) SAO gains 0.019 to 0.022 and BPCO 0.028 to 0.031 in Q-eval, against a noise floor of at most 0.0024 from the same recipes on outcomes flipped for a random half of the games (one seed each; the thread count alone moves SAO's winner gap by 0.0013 and its Q-eval by 0.0001; five seeds of every arm and floor give the same verdicts, in the eighth step below), with support mass within 0.52 points of the clone and the pessimistic estimate within 0.0007 of the restricted one. SAO masks 47% of its tokens, sharpens (entropy 2.72, KL 1.41) and raises the winner gap from 0.034 to 0.148, partly by being sharper in states that look won (the entropy gap of -0.059 alone is about half the rise; the card token's winner gap also rises, from 0.017 to 0.058); BPCO masks 6%, moves further (KL 2.28) while its entropy rises to 3.42, and its winner gap falls to 0.022 (the card token's rises from 0.017 to 0.030, so the fall is in the cell token) while its Q-eval rises most, so the two offline measures disagree about what was learnt. Unconstrained REINFORCE collapses (entropy 0.94, 4.6 points of mass off support) and is the only arm the registered rule flags. On each next unseen day, before training on it, the ordering is the same (on 5 September the clone scores 0.515, FlashREINFORCE 0.514, SAO 0.534, BPCO 0.544, the flipped arms 0.513 to 0.516; the default estimate was fit on all four stream days, so a scored day's actions, though not its outcomes, entered the ratios beforehand, and the estimate fit before the stream gives the same ordering: 0.519, 0.533 and 0.543), and the gains grow with data: BPCO 0.0007, 0.0099, 0.028 and 0.029 after 1,000, 10,000, 100,000 and all 217,370 games, SAO 0.0005, 0.0081, 0.018 and 0.020. The opponent's hand and the recorded-continuation verdict make the critic predict the outcome better without changing the policy (held-out explained variance 0.039 for the plain critic, 0.045 with the hand, 0.095 with the verdict); the no-play rollout is no better (on the 17,640 rolled-out games 0.033 plain, 0.034 with the rollout in one run, 0.036 with both), and the Q-eval gains stay within 0.001 of the plain critic's. The world model as an environment fares no better: the earlier group arm, replaying the menus of 400 of those games in the simulator (39,548 full replays, 107 CPU-seconds per game against 1.7 for a game's no-play rollouts), moves the policy by KL 0.02 and the Q-eval by 0.0004, inside the noise floor. The reading: from one trajectory per game and a behaviour estimate instead of stored probabilities, a trust region defined against the stored probabilities cannot be used, while token masks with a learnt critic extract an outcome signal that replicates on a later day, grows with data and saturates near 100,000 games; that signal is an association the held-out Q model reproduces, not a measured causal improvement, and at the simulator's current fidelity the world model adds nothing to it either as a critic input or as an environment.

Twenty tests cover the extractor (the rollout leaves the replay unchanged and restores the random state it consumes, forecasts a hog rider's tower damage and stops on an empty field; the opponent's hand is the replay's cycle; the selection drops modes and excluded ids) and the learners on synthetic games with a planted rule, a hidden opponent card and a planted rollout lead (the advantage estimator; the three losses, including the trajectory-level gate on the behaviour-first divergence, batch-mean centring and SAO's ratio weight; the critic's update count, rate and Monte Carlo target and the raw against whitened advantages; the bounded critic; the time order, warm-up-only standardisation and the Q model's half; one-pass learning of the rule with flipped outcomes as a floor, the closing gate, the privileged critics' explained variance, the pessimistic Q-eval against an extrapolating model, an oracle world model for the group arm, and a mask bug in the shared policy class, found and fixed in this round, that dropped the first vocabulary card from short hands in 539 records of this corpus). The third and fourth steps ran with that mask: on their 799,645 decisions it hid archer_queen (the first vocabulary card) in 35 short menus, 3 of them the recorded play, all in training and none held out, so their numbers stand without a rerun. Reports: `local/traceRlRound/rlScale/`.

The seventh step asks whether a world model learnt from the same traces is a better environment than the hand-built simulator, and whether group-relative training inside it beats the single-trajectory recipes. `train/learnedWm.py` fits, on the warm-up and stream games only, a model at decision resolution along one player's trajectory. A decision state is encoded to a latent vector; the actor's play is followed by the opponent's first play before the actor's next decision (a card, its cell and its delay, or none, which is the case in 34% of intervals), and the dynamics map latent, play and opponent play to the latent of the actor's next decision. Heads read from a latent a summary of the state (time, the elixir rate, the opponent's elixir, since the replay tops the actor's own up to ten at every recorded decision, crowns, the six towers' health, unit counts by owner, lane and half, and unit health by owner and half), the opponent's play and delay given the actor's play, the actor's next delay and whether the game ends before it, the actor's own play given its hand, and the outcome: a value of the latent and a one-step Q of latent and play. Training unrolls four decisions along the recorded trajectories with both sides' recorded plays and pulls each unrolled latent toward the encoding of the recorded next state (the consistency loss of EfficientZero, Ye et al. 2021, without its projector and predictor heads). The outcome heads are fit on encoded recorded states only: fit on unrolled latents as well, as in MuZero (Schrittwieser et al. 2020), they learn which recorded plays preceded a win, and in a planted test that confound reversed the multi-step model's verdict. Five members differ in seed; four drive the arms and the fifth is kept out to check exploitation. A rollout alternates the actor's plays (from heads distilled from the current policy at every update) and the model's opponent plays for H decisions, shares every sampled play across members so that their spread is the models' own, cycles the hand as the game does, ends softly by the done probability and bootstraps with the value. On 20,000 held-out decisions the model beats persistence at every step (standardised squared error of tower health 0.069 against 0.091 one decision ahead and 0.267 against 0.430 four ahead; unit counts 0.45 against 1.04 and 0.59 against 1.62), predicts the opponent's next card at 3.21 nats against 3.65 for its frequency and its cell at 2.32 against 3.59, the two delays to within 1.8 and 2.6 seconds against 2.1 and 4.0 for the median, and the end of the game at log loss 0.084 against 0.170; its one-step Q reaches log loss 0.667 on half B, where the evaluation's Q model, fit on half A, reaches 0.682.

The arms ran in the same single pass as the merged recipes, whose rerun reproduces the sixth step to four decimals. The group arm samples eight plays from the policy at a quarter of each batch's states, rolls each four decisions in the model, takes its value minus the group mean, scales by the batch spread and applies BPCO's binary-TV mask against the frozen clone; a variant in the style of MOPO (Yu et al. 2020) subtracts from the value 0.56 times the ensemble disagreement (the weight that equalises the two spreads on warm-up states); the one-step variant uses the members' Q without dynamics (H = 0); the noise floor refits the value and Q heads on outcomes flipped for half the games. Registered before the first run: the floor F is the largest flipped-outcome gain, an arm improves if it gains more than 2F, and it beats another only by more than F. The group arm gains 0.0309 in the pessimistic Q-eval (0.0308 for a second seed, whose shift agrees at cosine 0.997) at KL 4.6 and with 2.3 points less mass on plays seen 100 times; BPCO gains 0.0291 and SAO 0.0197; the MOPO variant 0.0186 at KL 1.9 with no loss of support; the one-step variant 0.0378 by collapsing (entropy 0.31, KL 8.4, its own Q rating its plays at 0.81); one dynamics step 0.0220 with support down 1.25 points, which the registered rule flags. The learned model's floor is 0.0124 for one flip seed and -0.0010 for another (KL 3.7 and 3.0, the two drifts agreeing at cosine 0.18; the second seed was added after the first floor was seen, and since F is the largest flipped gain it could only have raised F): scaled by the batch spread, the flipped heads' near-constant values (spread 0.006 against 0.11 for the real heads) become full-size steps in a random direction, so the floor is fifteen times BPCO's (0.0008). Against it the group arm does not beat BPCO (by 0.00185), multi-step dynamics add nothing measurable over the one-step Q (-0.0069), and neither anchor to a fixed clone bounds the drift in one pass: the binary-TV mask lets plays below probability 0.2 be pushed to zero, and with the ppo1 ratio clip the one-step variant still reaches KL 4.9 (that arm was added after the registration, on seeing the collapse; the four-step arm with the same clip, registered as optional, stays at KL 0.012 and gains 0.0014). The verdict on BPCO depends on which flip seed sets the floor: the margin of 0.00185 lies below the first seed's floor (0.01239) but above the second's (0.00098) and BPCO's own flipped floor (0.00079), against either of which it would count as beating BPCO, and the two seeds differ by 0.013, seven times the margin, so the group arm and BPCO are indistinguishable within the floor's seed spread. "Improves" is close in the same way: BPCO and the group arm clear 2F (0.0248) by 0.004 and 0.006, so a third flip seed with a floor above 0.0145 would remove BPCO and one above 0.0155 the group arm as well; settling either needs more flip seeds, which the eighth step below supplies (the two are indistinguishable over five seeds, and both improve against their own floors set by five flip seeds). As an environment the learned model is no better than the simulator. On the simulator's own 9,887 menus with the same clipped update its values give 0.0005 after 30 epochs, the simulator's 0.0004; its menu advantages agree with the held-out real-outcome Q model at cosine 0.062, the simulator's at 0.045 and its flipped copy's at 0.041 (the one-step Q's at 0.276); on 20,000 held-out decisions the four-step advantages agree at 0.051 against 0.043 flipped, and two halves of the ensemble agree with each other at 0.16. The policies trained in the model move toward the real-outcome recipes about as far as the more aligned flipped copy does (shift cosine 0.50 with BPCO and 0.54 with SAO; flipped copies -0.17 to 0.47 and 0.07 to 0.44; the simulator's menu arm -0.07 and 0.19). The exploitation check, as registered, continues each rollout with the behaviour (the model's actor heads) and passes narrowly: the group arm's favourite plays are rarer in the data (median count 5,558 against 13,828 for the clone's), and its in-model gain on held-out states is 0.049 under the training members and 0.045 under the kept-out member against its held-out gain of 0.031 (1.6 times, the threshold two). Under its own distilled continuation, the one it trains with, the arm's in-model value on the stream states rises from the clone's level (0.514, where the clipped four-step arm stays) to 0.669, a gain of 0.155 or about five times its held-out gain. By the registered measure the one-step variant gains 1.85 times its held-out gain and is not flagged; its own one-step Q rates the gain 7.9 times, but the same one-step measure rates BPCO, which never saw the model, at 4.4 times and the group arm at 7.2 times, so most of that ratio is the scale of the model's one-step Q rather than exploitation peculiar to the arm. The arm with one dynamics step is flagged (2.96 times by the registered measure, 2.93 at its own horizon). The reading: a model learnt from production traces predicts the next decision's state, the opponent's reply and the end of the game better than persistence and frequency, but its multi-step counterfactuals carry little more of the real-outcome direction than a copy with flipped outcomes, so group-relative training inside it reaches the single-trajectory token masks without beating them and moves further from the data to get there; here as before the Q-eval gains are associations that a held-out outcome model of the same states reproduces, not measured causal improvements.

Seven of nine tests drive the shipped functions on planted-dynamics games in which a giant takes tower health two decisions after it is played while a hidden handicap makes weaker players play it more, so the recorded outcomes rate it below the other cards: the summary reads a featurised simulator board from both sides; the auxiliary targets match the generator's own record of every game (the opponent's first reply with ties broken by replay order, the delays, the plays in between and the queue's next cards); the model beats persistence; the one-step Q and one dynamics step rate the giant below the other cards and only two learnt decisions rate it above them (advantage -0.16, -0.15 and +0.05); rollouts cycle the hand through the queue and identical members disagree by nothing; inside the model the group arm raises the giant's probability from 0.11 to 0.71 while the one-step variant drops it to 0.0003 and the flipped heads move it by 0.02, the exploitation measure scores that policy above the clone two decisions ahead and below it at one step, and a persisted preparation is restored exactly from its saved half clones; menus hold the recorded play and three distinct alternatives. The other two drive the shipped rollout and the group arm's advantages with hand-built members: in one the opponent's card alone decides whether the next state is worth 0.8 or 0.2, and rollouts of one and two decisions follow the sampled reply, which members that disagree share; in the other each play's value is its state's offset plus its card's effect, and the group arm's advantages are the value minus its group's mean over the batch spread (a hand-built pair of groups where a batch-mean baseline or none gives different advantages), unchanged when the offsets change. The ninth step asks whether those measures can be trusted at all. No policy trained on recorded games can be played in the real environment, so every verdict above rests on two proxies computed on held-out recorded games, the support-restricted Q-eval and the winner gap. The simulator can be played, so `train/simTruth.py` makes it the production world and repeats the procedure there (registered before any training run as hive finding f5369). The corpus clone, tempered to temperature 1.25 so that it explores, plays both sides of 78,000 games at decision resolution under rules shared by every policy: a player decides once a second has passed since its last play, a card is affordable, and its elixir has reached a threshold redrawn after each play or an enemy troop stands on its half; it picks a card from the affordable hand and one of the 48 deploy cells, with decks drawn from the 400 most played recorded decks. The games are written as corpus shards, one trajectory per player with the outcome as the only reward and the behaviour's probabilities stored beside them, and the shipped pack, prep and single-pass recipes train on them unchanged: 10,000 warm-up games, a stream of 60,000 consumed once, and 8,000 held-out games, half of them fitting the Q model. Because this world can be replayed exactly, environment-replay GRPO (Shao et al. 2024) is the upper bound: from one recorded decision of each stream game it restores the state, plays eight continuations to the end with the current policy against the behaviour, and weights every token of the actor by the outcome standardised within its group, with the simulated ticks the 60,000 stream games cost as its budget (it spent 0.49% more, because its last batch crossed the limit). Every policy then plays the same 10,000 seeded games (5,000 for floors, smaller volumes and references) against the behaviour and against a rule bot that beats the behaviour 64% of the time.

In this world the single-trajectory recipes work, and the Q-eval ranks them. The clone every arm starts from wins 48.9% against the behaviour; GRPO raises that by 17.7 points (95% interval 16.6 to 18.7) and was still climbing when its budget ran out (2.9, 4.9 and 9.5 points at an eighth, a quarter and half of it). Trained once through the traces with the estimated behaviour, ungated FlashREINFORCE gains 13.6 points, SAO 12.5 and BPCO 12.3, 70 to 77% of GRPO's gain for the same simulated ticks; gated FlashREINFORCE gains 4.6 (its gate admits about 30% of trajectories) and the online clone 0.7, inside its interval; the learned world model's four-step group arm gains 6.8 points and its one-step variant 20.2, more than GRPO, although its policy collapses (entropy 0.92). Against the bot the order is the same. The gains grow with the traces without saturating (BPCO 3.8, 8.8 and 12.3 points after 6,000, 20,000 and 60,000 games, SAO 2.0, 5.9 and 12.5), and per simulated tick BPCO is ahead of GRPO early (8.8 points after a third of the traces, against 4.9 and 9.5 for GRPO after a quarter and half of its budget) and behind at the end. Storing the behaviour's probabilities changes no recipe's gain by more than 1.6 points, because a clone estimates a behaviour of its own architecture closely (mean Bernoulli divergence 0.0018 per token from the stored probabilities, below the gate's 0.003, where on the corpus the estimate sat 0.010 from the actor's starting point); by the registered rule, a paired interval that excludes zero, it still matters in 4 of the 8 comparisons, all in favour of storing (SAO against both opponents, both FlashREINFORCE arms against the bot). Flipped-outcome copies gain nothing against the behaviour (minus 1.4 to plus 0.3 points) and lose 1.4 to 2.5 points against the bot. Over the 26 trained policies the gain in the support-restricted Q-eval has rank correlation 0.995 with the true gain against the behaviour (2.5th percentile 0.9747 in a game bootstrap over the 5,000 games every policy played) and 0.987 against the bot, its top policy is the true top, and the registered verdict built on it (a gain above twice the flipped floor with support kept) agrees with the true verdict for 24 of the 26, missing two true gains of 2 to 3 points; the winner gap moves the other way (rank correlation minus 0.58), falling most for the policies that win most. How far a policy moved already ranks them at 0.88 (KL to the clone), since nearly every outcome-driven arm moves in a winning direction; the flipped copies, which move without winning, are where the Q-eval earns its place. Two differences bound the transfer to recorded games: the synthetic outcome is far more predictable from the decision state (the held-out Q model is right 76% of the time against 57% on the corpus), so the Q-eval has more to rank with, and the behaviour is one fixed clone, easy to estimate, not a population of players. Two limits bound the comparisons themselves. Each arm was trained with one seed, and the eighth step found that the training seed can decide a comparison, so the one-step variant's lead over GRPO (2.6 points, 95% interval 1.6 to 3.6) and the two 2-to-3-point gains the registered verdict misses each rest on a single seed. GRPO's hyperparameters were fixed in advance, not tuned, so it is an upper bound only for this recipe at this budget. Seven tests drive the shipped functions: cells correspond across the two sides and land on deployable tiles, and an enemy troop on a player's half makes it decide below its elixir threshold; a game is reproduced by replaying its plays from the store's float32 times, past 32 s where they differ from the tick times by more than 1e-6, and a GRPO group starts from the recorded state with the recorded menu, the actor playing that side and the behaviour the other; an evaluation game seats the policy blue with the first deck on even seeds and red with it on odd seeds; the GRPO surrogate has the policy-gradient gradient at the sampling policy, and the paired interval spans 1.96 standard errors; the report applies the registered rules to hand-built win files and run reports with known answers (a flipped copy that sets the floor, a gain between the floor and twice it, a gain that loses support, known rank correlations, interval width and recovery ratio); in a planted world where the right card is written in the state and the winner is drawn from the two sides' counts of right plays, generation, pack, prep, the stored probabilities, run_arms, GRPO and the win-rate evaluation raise the true win rate by more than 8 points for BPCO, ungated FlashREINFORCE and GRPO while the critic-free recipe's flipped copy gains nothing (BPCO's flipped copy keeps part of BPCO's gain there through the critic pretrained on true warm-up outcomes); and games pair across agents and an interrupted evaluation resumes. The tenth step asks the question a recorded corpus actually poses: could a world model learnt from the traces have stood in for the simulator in that evaluation? `train/wmOpe.py` evaluates the ninth step's 26 trained policies and their clone from the traces alone and checks every estimate against the true win rate against the behaviour, from the first decisions of the 16,000 held-out trajectories (registered as hive finding f5472 before any estimate). In the seventh step's learned world model, refit on the first 10,000, 30,000 or 70,000 games with ensembles of one, four or eight members, a policy makes its first play on the recorded state and its later plays through a copy distilled into each member's latent space (at most 0.042 nats from the policy), the model supplies the opponent, the timing and the end of the game, and the value after k decisions adds the model's value of the games still running, from k = 0 (the one-step outcome model) to 64 (a full game); the model has no hand, so the actor's recorded deck cycles and the menu holds the cards affordable at the recorded elixir. The four-member 70,000-game ensemble is the one the ninth step trained both of its learned-model arms in, the group arm through rollouts of four decisions and its one-step variant through the members' one-step outcome estimate (k = 0); the 10,000- and 30,000-game members were fit separately, but from the same initial weights as those four and on the first games of the same traces. Beside it run fitted-Q evaluation (Le, Voloshin and Yue 2019), which regresses the policy's value backwards through 32 rounds on the same traces, and importance sampling with its doubly robust forms (Jiang and Li 2016; Thomas and Brunskill 2016), using the behaviour's stored probabilities; in deep off-policy evaluation benchmarks the first two are among the strongest estimators (Fu et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2021). Full rollouts in the 70,000-game model get the level right (the clone's win rate 49.1% against a true 48.9%) and rank the policies with Spearman correlation 0.947, but every gain comes out at about half its true size, and the four-step group arm is rated 12.2 points above the clone against a true 6.8 and ranked first, 13.4 points below the true best (its one-step variant), so the registered rule rejects the configuration. Three post hoc analyses (not registered) place that overrating: without the four-step arm the rollouts' gains are proportional to the truth (slope 0.50, mean error 0.7 points after rescaling); the arm's ratio of estimated to true gain grows with the rollout's length (0.2 at k = 0, 1.8 at 64); and against each evaluator's own scale, its slope over the other 25 policies, every one-step evaluator rates the one-step variant about two and a half times too high (the one-step model 2.64 times, fitted-Q evaluation's starting outcome model 2.81, the Q-eval 2.36) while rating the four-step arm like the rest or lower (0.66 to 1.02), and every evaluator of four or more decisions rates the four-step arm about three to five and a half times too high (2.65 to 5.74: model rollouts of 4 decisions, and of 64 in every volume and ensemble, and fitted-Q evaluation after 32 rounds, which is no world model, 3.13), while rollouts of 1 and 2 decisions and fitted-Q evaluation after 1 to 4 rounds rate it only 1.23 to 1.93 times. So a policy optimised against an evaluator of a given horizon is overrated by evaluators of that horizon, whichever class they belong to; the 10,000- and 30,000-game members overrate the four-step arm even more on that scale, so they are no independent check (members with new seeds alone were not evaluated). More traces mainly fix the level (44.8% for the clone at 10,000 games); more members do not improve the ranking (0.957, 0.947 and 0.946 with one, four and eight), but one member picks the one-step variant where four and eight pick the four-step arm; and the ensemble-disagreement penalty of model-based offline RL (Yu et al. 2020) at the weight its training arms used destroys the ranking (0.58), because disagreement accumulates over a game and grows with the distance from the behaviour. The one-step model ranks better (0.983) and picks the true best, at a fifth of the true gains. Fitted-Q evaluation ranks 0.87 to 0.90 with its gains about half size; importance weights over 30-decision games leave an effective sample under 40 of 16,000 for half the policies, and at 70,000 games the doubly robust estimates rank below 0.5 with the stored probabilities (0.57 and 0.66 at best at 10,000 and 30,000 games; 0.84 at best with an estimated behaviour, which trades that variance for bias). Only the secondary configuration, whose menus replay the recorded trajectory, passes the registered rule (at 32 and 64 decisions; at 64 it ranks 0.949 with a mean gain error of 2.1 points and picks the true best), so the registered overall verdict is that model-based evaluation helps, but only through that secondary configuration. It is fragile: a card the policy favours can recur, in a planted test it rates such a policy above both its true win rate and the full rollouts' estimate, and it still rates the group arm at 2.2 times its true gain. Nothing matches the Q-eval's 0.995. For the recorded corpus this means a learned model can supply what the Q-eval cannot, an absolute win rate for policies near the behaviour and gains in the right proportions though of unknown scale, but no learned evaluator tested here, world model or not, can be trusted with a policy optimised against a learned evaluator of its own horizon. Ranking with held-out one-step outcome models picked the true best here only because the policy they overrate, the one-step variant, really is the best (20.2 points against GRPO's 17.7, intervals disjoint); the evidence is two policies with one training seed each, and a policy trained in a learned model needs the simulator or real held-out play to be certified. The synthetic outcome's predictability (held-out accuracy 0.76 against 0.57 on the corpus) flatters every outcome-based evaluator here. Nine tests drive the shipped functions on planted worlds with known policy values: both doubly robust estimates are exact given the policy's exact Q and importance sampling is consistent; fitted-Q round k equals the binomial value of the policy for k + 1 decisions followed by the behaviour; a rollout's value at a mark equals the rollout cut there; the value at k = 0 is the members' one-step outcome estimate of the policy's own first play; the penalty weights each predicted decision's disagreement by the chance that the game reaches it (not at all when every game ends at the first decision, by one half to the power j + 1 at decision j when each ends with probability one half); recorded menus give decision k the menu of the k-th next recorded decision; the deck cycle and affordable menus behave as in the game; the registered comparison picks inside and outside the true best's interval; and in a planted world where a giant played now damages a tower later, full model rollouts and fitted-Q evaluation order the never, uniform and always-giant policies as their true win rates (0.39, 0.53, 0.60) while recorded menus rate the always-giant policy above its true win rate and above the full rollouts' estimate. The eleventh step asks how a policy trained on production traces could then be certified without the environment, given that a learned evaluator overrates a policy trained against a learned evaluator of its own horizon. `train/certify.py` tests certification rules, registered before any fit as hive finding f5520, on the same 26 policies and true win rates, from the first decisions of the 8,000 held-out B trajectories with a paired bootstrap over their games. Beside the tenth step's evaluators it fits new ones: world-model members with new seeds and fitted-Q evaluation on the held-out A games, which no policy, critic or training-time evaluator saw (and the same on half B, scored from half A, as a replication), the 70,000-game model's four other members, and the Q-eval per record. A combined rule divides each evaluator's gains by its mean gain over the 24 policies trained without a learned evaluator, which needs no truth, and leaves out an evaluator whose mean gain over them is not positive (this happened once, to the replication's fitted-Q evaluation at eight rounds, in its split mean); a policy is certified when its score minus 1.645 bootstrap standard deviations is positive; and the overrating ratio compares a policy's score with those 24 policies' scores relative to their true gains (1 means rated like them). No remedy removes the overrating by itself. Evaluators fit on the unseen half overrate the four-step arm at long horizons as much as its own training ensemble does (full rollouts 3.95 times, 4.94 in the replication; fitted-Q evaluation 2.57 and 2.53) and the one-step arm at one step (3.3 and 3.2 times, 3.0 and 1.5 in the replication; the Q-eval, itself fit on held-out games, 2.36), and new members on the same traces rate the four-step arm 4.06 times. The registered split fits reuse the 70,000-game step counts on a 4,000-game half and overfit (the outcome model under their fitted-Q evaluation has a log loss of .549 on half B and .515 on half A, against .473 for the ninth step's Q, also fit on half A, on the same half-B records), but fits stopped early, added as an exploratory check before any comparison with the truth (hive finding f5521), overrate the four-step arm as much (rollouts 4.84, fitted-Q evaluation 3.20). So the overrating is not exploitation of particular members or of noise in the shared traces but a bias that learned evaluators of the matching horizon share, and a policy optimised against one inherits it. A mismatched horizon helps only at the opposite extreme (from the B starts the one-step evaluators rate the four-step arm 0.43 to 1.82 times, those of 32 or more decisions the one-step arm 0.76 to 2.24 times, single members included), so leaving out only the training horizon changes nothing (2.87 and 2.07), and the one-step evaluators fit on half B overrate the four-step arm too (about 3 times). The minimum over model classes (one-step outcome models, full model rollouts, fitted-Q evaluation) lowers the ratios to 1.64 and 1.42 (1.64 and 1.33 from the A starts); with the split fits and the Q-eval in its one-step family it reaches 1.25 and 0.97, while the replication's version, which cannot include the Q-eval (fit on half A) and so is not the same rule, gives 2.85 and 1.15. The minimum over all fifteen same-data evaluators is the only registered rule that meets the registered certification criterion from both halves' start states, but these are the same 70,000-game members and fitted-Q fits scored from the B and from the A starts, so the second half is not an out-of-sample replication of the rule: it ranks the policies at 0.944 and 0.952, picks the true best, and certifies 17 of the 21 true improvers and no other policy, yet still rates the four-step arm 1.8 times; no registered rule both meets that criterion and avoids the overrating (a ratio of at most 1.5 for both arms). The Q-eval ranks best (0.995) and picks the true best, but certifies the online clone and FlashREINFORCE on 6,000 games, whose true gains (0.7 and 1.3 points) have intervals that include zero. A bootstrap over start states also cannot see an evaluator's own fitting error, which the overfit half-data fits enlarge, so they certify the flipped-outcome copies. For production traces this means that no learned evaluator tried here can certify a policy trained against a learned evaluator at its estimated size; the minimum over every horizon and class bounds the damage (the right pick, no false certificate, an overrating of 1.8 rather than 3 to 5) and is the screen to use, with the simulator or real held-out play for the claim, though this recommendation is in-sample (the tenth step's analysis of the same evaluators motivated the rule). The family rules were motivated by the tenth step's analysis of the same estimates, so only the new fits are out-of-sample evidence, and the evidence is two learned-evaluator policies with one training seed each. Eight tests drive the shipped functions: they pin the registered rule set as written in f5520, judge every registered rule on planted gains with known overrating, and include a planted world in which a policy trained against one trace half's outcome model is rated best by that half's evaluators at every horizon while it truly loses more often than the behaviour, and the clean half's evaluators and the minimum over both halves neither pick nor certify it. Reports: `local/certifyRound/`.

## 6. Comparison arms and evaluation

All arms share one evaluation: win rate in the simulator against fixed opponents (a rule bot, the behavior-cloned policy, and each other), plus next-action accuracy on human games with a battle-level split, so no battle appears in both training and evaluation.

Arm 1 is corrected offline reinforcement learning. Earlier results in this repository were contaminated by leakage between training and evaluation; the arm redoes them with the battle-level split, implicit Q-learning (Kostrikov, Nair and Levine 2022) or conservative Q-learning (Kumar et al. 2020) on the human data, then PPO (Schulman et al. 2017) self-play in the simulator.

Arm 2 is a zero-shot language-model agent through the xAI API (environment variable `XAI_API_KEY`, base URL `https://api.x.ai/v1`, OpenAI-compatible, default model `grok-4.5`; current names are at https://docs.x.ai/developers/models). At each decision point it receives a textual state (time, own elixir, hand, tower hit points, entity list with positions, the opponent's revealed cards) and returns a card and tile or a wait; about 40 calls per game make a few hundred evaluation games affordable. The same interface can ask the model for the opponent's elixir and next card, which scores its explicit world model without probes.

Arm 3 is a small open model, Qwen3 1.7B or Llama 3.2 3B, fine-tuned with low-rank adaptation (Hu et al. 2022) on serialized state-action pairs from the human data. Linear probes on its residual stream for opponent elixir and the opponent's next card test whether it forms an implicit world model, in the style of the Othello probes of Section 3; because the card cycle is a known finite state machine, the compression and distinction tests of Vafa et al. (2024) apply to it directly.

Arm 4 is the grid learner with the planner of Section 5.

Interpretability applies to arms 3 and 4. Probes for hidden variables measure what each model represents. Channel ablations (zeroing the geometry channel, the hit point channel, the opponent's global tokens) find which inputs carry each prediction. Distillation of the learned kernel per mechanic fits small symbolic rules, for example a decision tree for a unit's target switch as a function of the local distances, to the kernel's behavior on synthetic cells. And a loop feeds disagreements back into the simulator: where a kernel trained on human replays predicts something the coded rule does not, one of them is wrong, and the check of Section 7 decides which.

## 7. Data and fidelity

Replay collection and datasets remain in the private scraping repository. Evaluation uses recorded placements, card levels, deck variants, results, crowns, and final tower hit points. Samples must be matched to the balance period and game mode, with duplicate battles excluded across evaluation splits. Collection throughput and completeness require measurement rather than assumptions about service limits.

The fidelity program compares recorded human placements against simulated winners, crowns, final tower hit points, early endings, and nearby units at selected spell casts. These are separate diagnostic measures, not an overall accuracy percentage. Invalid recorded placements are reported as relocated or skipped; replay recovery must not fabricate tower destruction. Calibration logs carry content fingerprints for the engine, parser, card data, and replay inputs so results from different baselines cannot silently mix. Mechanics fixes require sourced behavior, regression tests, and before/after replay checks. Each main reassessment uses a new sample of exactly 2,000 eligible games, disjoint from all previous evaluation samples. Selection and eligibility are frozen before simulation, and both versions run on that same sample. A reported result requires all 2,000 unique games to complete with matching input hashes; smaller or card-filtered subsets are diagnostic only. Exact trajectory fidelity still requires measurements from recent gameplay recordings.

Checked and left unchanged: open-loop replay as the carrier of the crown excess. Hidden units are now untargetable on every targeting path, which closes the gap the evolved drill round left open. The [Royal Ghost page](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Royal_Ghost) says that while he is invisible "he will not be targeted by opposing units, but can still be hit by area damage or spells", that "An Electro Dragon or Electro Spirit's attack can also chain onto him", that "any projectiles that have already been fired to target him will deal damage to him", and that when he hides "enemy troops will stop chasing him"; the [Archer Queen](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Archer_Queen) is "untargetable by enemy troops" under her Cloaking Cape, the [Suspicious Bush](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Suspicious_Bush) is "completely untargetable to opposing troops", and the Ghost's and the Queen's histories record a fix of 8 April 2022 after which invisible units take splash damage. For a unit underground the [Tesla page](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Tesla) states the rule (it "cannot be targeted by any troops" and is "invulnerable to any damage-dealing spell except for Earthquake"), and the [Little Prince](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Little_Prince) history records a fix that stopped his Royal Rescue hitting troops underground. Neither drill page says in words that the submerged evolved drill cannot be targeted: the export calls the submerge a hide, and the evolution page only notes that its resurfaces make the Crown Towers retarget. The engine counts a unit as hidden when it is invisible (the Ghost, the cloaked Queen, the Bush, the Boss Bandit after her grenade, the evolved Lumberjack's ghost, the evolved Minion Horde's veil and the evolved Skeleton Army's shadows) or burrowed (a travelling Miner or Goblin Drill and the submerged evolved drill). That branch now drops a target that hides, and every attacker forgets it instead of taking it up again when it reappears. The attacks that choose their own target passed over nothing: the Electro Wizard's second target, the Goblin Machine's rocket, the Golden Knight's dash, the Little Prince's rescue, the hero Giant's hurl, the hero Mega Minion's warp, the hero Balloon's cadets, the hero Valkyrie's whirlwind and the evolved Goblin Cage's trap. Chain bounces (Electro Dragon, Electro Spirit, the evolved Electro Dragon's bolt) are the exception the page names: they still jump onto invisible units and now skip only units underground. Lightning and Vines, which pick the highest-hitpoint bodies in their radius, likewise skip units underground but still strike invisible ones (the [Lightning page](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Lightning) says it "can hit the Archer Queen even with her ability active"). Area damage is unchanged: splash, spells and death blasts hit invisible units, burrowed units stay immune, and a projectile or a dash already under way still lands; the evolved Bomber's bounce is left as it is, since in the game it is a forward area blast rather than a choice of target. Targeting now keeps a target only while it is in attack range. The [Basics of Battle](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Basics_of_Battle) page says troops engage the nearest enemy in sight and "once engaged, the troop will automatically follow and attack that target as long as possible"; on that sentence the engine held a troop target at any distance for as long as it lived and stayed visible, while buildings, building-targeting troops and any unit aimed at a tower already kept their target only while it was in range. The card pages describe the limit of "as long as possible": the [Mega Knight](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Mega_Knight) page says "similar to the Bandit, the Mega Knight will re-target onto any troop placed nearby him if his original target ends up being farther away than the newly spawned troop", a sentence about his target in the moment before his jump (the page goes on to advise a cheap troop "in front of him right before he is about to jump"), the Mini P.E.K.K.A page that he "may retarget" onto Skeletons or Goblins accompanying a Hog Rider, the [Battle Ram](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Battle_Ram) page that slower chasers "will eventually lose sight of the Battle Ram and will not be kited the whole way", and the [Little Prince](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Little_Prince) page that Spear Goblins "locked onto the Little Prince can be pushed out of range by the Royal Rescue knockback, causing them to retarget", while the [Inferno Dragon](https://clashroyale.fandom.com/wiki/Inferno_Dragon) page says a Tornado "will not retarget" it while its target stays in range. Short engine scenarios showed the three contradictions (a walking Mega Knight, P.E.K.K.A or Prince kept a troop 2.6 tiles ahead with Skeletons touching him; a P.E.K.K.A chased a charging Battle Ram 8 tiles away until it died; a Knight knocked 2 tiles back kept the farther of two troops) and agreement elsewhere: troops walking to a tower take Skeletons in sight, a unit hitting a troop or a tower keeps it, stun and freeze make it choose again, a Cannon or Bomb Tower at the wiki's spot 4 tiles from the river and 2 from the tower pulls a Hog Rider, Giant, Battle Ram and Mega Knight, and an Ice Golem kites slow chasers across the river within their sight. The two tournament recordings contain no kite and no Bandit; the one 1080p reading of a unit holding a troop out of reach while another stood beside it (a Baby Dragon at 104 to 107 s of the first game) agrees with the new rule but does not separate the two. Every held target now holds only while it is in attack range (between the unit's minimum and maximum range, including while its first swing loads), and out of reach a unit takes the nearest target in sight each tick, which keeps its target while it stays the nearest, ends a chase out of sight and hands the unit to a nearer troop; the unsourced calibration knobs for a kiting drop were removed. Composed with the hidden-target rule above, the hold is one condition for every attacker: a target is kept while it is in attack range and not hidden, and a target that hides is forgotten. How high can winner agreement go under this judge? A diagnostic round (`local/ceilingRound/findings.md`, no engine or judge change) replayed one fresh 2,000-game sample at f21f354 nine times each under the noise the recording cannot pin down: other random seeds (13 draw sites, mostly spawn offsets), every placement moved by one tick of 0.05 seconds (the recording's resolution), and the corner-placed buildings by half a tile. Two runs under all three perturbations agree on the simulated winner 84.0% of the time (84.7% on a second sample); 40% of games flip in at least one run, mostly close one-crown games, tiebreaks, overtime and games over 180 seconds; with every random draw fixed, the one-tick shift alone still flips 34%, so the timing sensitivity is the engine's own. If the real game is as sensitive, a single replay can reach about 84% agreement with the recorded winners (about 80% if every flipping game is a coin flip), so the 70% target is well inside the ceiling and the gap from about 65% is systematic: 16.6% of games are wrong in all nine perturbed runs. Recorded agreement is 72.2% on the stable games and 55.5% on the flipping ones, and reaching 70% with the flipping games unchanged needs about 81% on the stable ones. Premature endings, where the simulated game ends more than a second before the last recorded play (about half of all games), flip no more often than other games but agree with the recorded winner far less often (57.9% against 73.1%), which marks them, and the surplus tower kills behind them, as the main systematic error. Two caveats: the fixed seed 42 that the judge uses scored above the mean of eight other seeds on all four samples tried (by 0.48 points on average, about 1.7 standard errors), so published levels may run about half a point high, and a single 2,000-game measurement carries about 1.2 points of combined judge and sampling noise, which the 63.8 to 67.8% spread of the fresh samples matches.

## 8. Roadmap

Phase 0 starts collection with the unified scraper and the official API client, establishes the replay harness metrics, and runs arms 1 and 2, which are cheap and give the baselines.

Phase 1 trains the kernel at k = 1 on simulator rollouts under scripted policies, reports per-cell exactness and the geometry-generalization check, and trains the observer against hidden state.

Phase 2 adds the step-size conditioning and consistency term, measures eps_k and L_F per k, builds the planner, and measures the planning gap against the oracle.

Phase 3 moves the kernel to human replays, runs arm 3 with its probes, and starts the distillation loop.

Phase 4 trains on the agent's own games and runs the full comparison across the four arms.
